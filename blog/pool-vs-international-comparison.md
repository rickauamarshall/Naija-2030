# The Talent Was Never the Problem: Three Levers, and the One Nigeria Isn't Pulling

*A Super Eagles Tracker feature. Independent. Not affiliated with the NFF, CAF, or FIFA.*

Issue №2 of this newsletter promised a full breakdown of an argument we could only sketch in a few paragraphs: that Nigeria's problem was never the depth of its talent pool, it's the absence of anyone running a deliberate process against that pool. We used two examples there — Mauricio Pochettino's USMNT evaluation marathon and Roberto Mancini's *oriundi* pipeline at Italy — and promised more. Here's the fuller version, with a third case study added, because two examples make a pattern feel neat and a third one is usually what breaks the neatness in a useful way.

The short version of where we land: there isn't one lever a federation pulls to fix a talent-to-results gap. There are at least three, they operate on different timelines, and they're not substitutes for each other. Nigeria, on the evidence available to us, isn't systematically pulling any of them. That's the actual finding here — not "Nigeria has no talent," which is trivially false, but "Nigeria has no process," which is a different and more fixable problem, if anyone in Abuja decides to treat it as one.

## Lever one: evaluation discipline (Pochettino, USMNT)

Start with the number that made us sit up in the first place. Mauricio Pochettino evaluated more than 60 candidates for the USMNT across 19 months, 8 training camps, and 24 matches before he named his final 26 for this summer's World Cup. That's not a scouting trip. That's a standing, funded, multi-year evaluation program with a paper trail — camps convened specifically to test fringe players against real opposition, not just to prepare the starting XI.

What that buys you isn't more talent. The USMNT player pool didn't get bigger because Pochettino watched more of it. What it buys you is fewer bad surprises and fewer wasted call-ups — you walk into a tournament knowing, with real match minutes behind the judgment, who your 27th-best player actually is instead of guessing from reputation or a scout's secondhand report.

Nigeria's equivalent apparatus, as far as we can tell from the outside, is Chelle's actual matchday squad-naming — which is real work, but it isn't the same category of thing. Compare the last two windows on our own board: the AFCON qualifying squad against Madagascar and Guinea-Bissau brought in three completely uncapped debutants — Isaac James, George Ilenikhena, Moses Usor — and the Russia friendly four days later brought in three *different* uncapped debutants — Victory Akpe, Samuel James, Abdullahi Bewene. Six new names in one week, and every one of them arrived on our board *after* the NFF's own squad list did, not before. We're a tracker built on the premise that we know this pool; the honest disclosure is that the coach's own selection sheet is currently a faster source of new information than we are, which tells you something about how thin the public evaluation record actually is. If there's a standing camp-and-match program feeding Chelle's staff the way Pochettino's staff fed him, it isn't visible from the outside, and a federation that wants credit for depth needs to be able to show its work the way the USMNT can.

## Lever two: recruitment speed (Mancini, Italy — and it's happening to Nigeria right now)

The second lever isn't about evaluating players already in the system — it's about getting to eligible players *before* another federation does. Roberto Mancini built exactly this at Italy: a deliberate, sustained pipeline chasing *oriundi*, dual nationals with Italian ancestry playing outside Italy, mostly out of Argentina and Brazil. Mateo Retegui is the headline case — developed entirely in Argentine football, qualified through a grandmother, and secured for the Azzurri by a federation that moved on him early and methodically rather than waiting for a moment of hesitation to close.

Here's the part that should actually worry Nigerian football-watchers rather than just interest them: Italy is running that exact playbook against Nigeria-eligible players right now, and our own Big Board has the receipts. Honest Ahanor, 18, on loan at Crystal Palace from Chelsea, has already taken two senior friendly caps for Italy — a substitute appearance against Luxembourg, a start against Greece. He's still eligible for FIFA's one-time-switch rule (under 21, three or fewer caps, no competitive minutes), which is exactly the kind of live, time-bound mechanism that a real recruitment operation would be tracking on a calendar, not discovering after the fact. Jeff Ekhator, 19, at Juventus, already has a senior Italy cap too — a June 7, 2026 debut against Greece where he recorded an assist. Same mechanism, same clock. Luca Koleosho and Michael Kayode are reportedly next in that same pipeline as Italy rebuilds. Four names, one federation, one deliberate strategy — and it's the same strategy Mancini industrialized on South American talent, now pointed at Nigerian-eligible players in Italy's own academy system.

Italy isn't winning these players on talent identification. Ahanor and Ekhator aren't obscure — they're both getting real minutes at Crystal Palace and Juventus respectively, which is exactly the kind of player any federation would want. Italy is winning on *speed*: camps, caps, and paperwork moving faster than any competing federation's outreach. And the eligibility clock genuinely doesn't wait — Rio Ngumoha, 17, at Liverpool, is the case on our board that shows exactly how fast the door can close. He made a senior England debut in a friendly against New Zealand in June 2026, which didn't cap-tie him. But Thomas Tuchel has since called him into England's *competitive* Nations League squad — and three appearances there permanently ends the conversation. That's not a hypothetical scenario playing out somewhere abstract. That's a clock running on a specific 17-year-old, right now, while we write this.

Not every one of these cases is moving Nigeria's way, and we're not going to pretend the pipeline record is clean. Nnamdi Collins made his senior competitive debut for Germany against Slovakia in September 2025 and is now permanently cap-tied — a real, finished loss. Carney Chukwuemeka, born in Vienna to Nigerian parents and genuinely in the conversation at one point, completed a FIFA-approved switch to Austria in March 2026 and has already debuted for them. Those two are gone. Against that, there's at least one clean win on the board: Arthur Okonkwo, the Wrexham goalkeeper born in Camden to Nigerian parents, had his switch approved by FIFA on April 6, 2026, after paperwork delays cost him the 2025 AFCON — proof the mechanism works when someone actually drives it to completion.

And then there's the larger group of cases sitting in genuine limbo, which is arguably the more damning category, because limbo is what a real pipeline would resolve one way or the other instead of leaving open indefinitely: Tosin Adarabioyo, 28 at Chelsea, has reportedly said yes to Nigeria more than once since 2024 and then gone quiet each time, with no active contact as of mid-2026 — a real case that's just stalled. Ethan Nwaneri, 19, on loan at Marseille from Arsenal, is an England U21 international but uncapped at senior level, and his family has reportedly opened the door to NFF talks — reportedly, not confirmed, and we're keeping that distinction because that's the standard we hold ourselves to. David Ozoh at Derby County reportedly agreed to switch after meeting the coaching staff, and doesn't even need FIFA clearance, just a release letter from the English FA — a bureaucratic step, not a legal one, and it's still not done. Lesley Ugochukwu is trending the *wrong* way: named in France's U21 squad on September 18, 2026, despite reports in July that he'd started pursuing a Nigerian passport, with no official change-of-association step actually taken. We moved him down our own board over it rather than pretend the momentum hadn't shifted.

This is what "no deliberate pipeline" looks like in practice: real, eligible, sometimes willing players, sitting in a queue that nobody is visibly managing, while Italy runs a calendar against at least four comparable cases and gets a debut cap logged before Nigeria gets a phone call returned.

## Lever three: structural reform after hitting bottom (Germany, post–Euro 2000)

The first two levers are about *process* and *speed* on the players you can already see. The third is different in kind: it's about rebuilding the system that produces players in the first place, and it's the one with the longest fuse.

Germany's version of this is the most-cited academy-reform story in modern football, and it's worth getting the actual shape of it right rather than repeating the folklore version. At Euro 2000, West Germany's successor team finished bottom of its group, took a single point from three matches, and was eliminated after the group stage — a genuine low point for a federation that had reached the previous World Cup final in 1990 and won it in 1990 and 1974 and 1954. That result functioned as the shock that made structural reform politically possible, the way a bad tournament sometimes has to happen before a federation will actually spend money on something that won't pay off for a decade.

What changed, in the two years that followed, was structural rather than cosmetic. Starting around 2001, the DFB and the Bundesliga made it a *licensing condition* for professional clubs to operate a certified, centrally regulated youth academy — a club without one risked losing the right to play in the top divisions at all, which is a genuinely different kind of pressure than a federation merely encouraging clubs to invest in youth. Alongside that, in 2002, the DFB stood up a nationwide network of regional talent centers — sources differ on the exact count and the year it's measured (figures we've seen range from roughly 120 regional performance centers up to a later network on the order of 350–390 smaller talent bases), but the stated design goal was consistent across sources: elite coaching within about 25 kilometers of essentially every youth player in the country, backed by well over a thousand DFB-employed coaches and a combined DFB-and-league annual investment reported at around €48 million. We're flagging the exact figures as approximate because our source material disagreed on the specific counts by year and we weren't able to independently pin down a single authoritative number in the time we had — the shape of the reform (mandatory licensing plus a funded national scouting-and-coaching grid) is well corroborated even where the headline numbers vary by source.

The payoff was not fast, and that's the actual lesson, not a footnote to it. Germany's golden generation — Philipp Lahm, Bastian Schweinsteiger, Miroslav Klose, Lukas Podolski and later Mesut Özil, Thomas Müller, Toni Kroos, Mario Götze — started coming through the system in visible form around the mid-2000s, made a genuinely young, attractive run to the semifinal at the 2006 home World Cup, reached the Euro 2008 final, the 2010 World Cup semifinal, and the Euro 2012 semifinal, before finally winning the World Cup in 2014 — with Götze, a direct product of the post-reform academy pipeline, scoring the winner. Call it roughly a decade to a full trophy, with the first visible signs of a deeper, younger pool showing up four to six years in. That's the tradeoff structural reform makes: it's the only one of the three levers that doesn't just find or manage existing talent, it changes how much good talent exists in the first place — and it's also the one lever no federation can fake with a press release, because you can't shortcut a 25-kilometer coaching grid or a mandatory academy-licensing requirement into existing by next season.

One more thing worth noting, since it sits right in our own data: Jamal Musiala, born in Stuttgart to a Nigerian father and a German mother, chose Germany over Nigeria in 2021 and is now a senior German international. That's not evidence Germany is running an Italy-style poaching operation aimed at Nigerian-eligible players specifically — nothing in our research suggests that's a deliberate federation strategy the way the *oriundi* pipeline explicitly is at Italy. It's a quieter, second-order effect of lever three: when your domestic academy system is simply the best developmental pathway a promising teenager can access, you don't need to recruit dual nationals aggressively, because the players raised inside your system choose to stay. Structural reform doesn't just plug leaks — eventually it stops water finding its way out in the first place.

## Which lever is Nigeria actually pulling?

Put the three side by side and the differences in timeline and mechanism matter as much as the differences in outcome:

- **Pochettino's lever** is evaluation discipline against players already inside the pool — the payoff is measured in months, and it mainly reduces the risk of a wrong final-squad decision rather than growing the pool itself.
- **Mancini's lever** is recruitment speed against dual nationals outside the pool — the payoff can be a single completed transfer of allegiance in a matter of months, but the window is often only open for a year or two per player before a rival federation or a competitive cap closes it for good.
- **Germany's lever** is structural reform of the system that produces the pool in the first place — the payoff takes the better part of a decade, but it's the only lever that makes the other two easier every year afterward, because there's simply more good talent for an evaluation process to sort through and fewer contested dual nationals a rival federation gets to first.

On the evidence in front of us, Nigeria isn't running a visible, sustained version of any of the three. There's no public multi-camp evaluation record comparable to Pochettino's — new names keep entering the squad from the coach's own selection sheet rather than from a standing scouting apparatus with a paper trail. There's no visible recruitment pipeline racing Italy's clock on Ahanor, Ekhator, Koleosho, and Kayode — the pattern on our own board is federations moving first (Italy, Germany, Austria, France) and Nigeria reacting after a cap or a squad call-up has already happened, with the Okonkwo case standing out precisely because it's the exception where someone clearly did drive the paperwork through. And there's no structural-reform story on the scale of Germany's post-2000 academy overhaul that we're aware of, despite Nigeria missing the last two World Cups in a row — arguably its own version of the shock that should make that kind of investment politically possible, the same way Euro 2000 did for Germany.

None of that means the talent isn't there. It plainly is — that's the entire premise this tracker runs on, and our own pool data backs it up without needing to reach for a single hypothetical: a 26-man formation depth chart plus a 12-entry ranked board carrying real, current, club-verified players and situations, not invented ones. It means the gap between Nigeria's results and Nigeria's player pool isn't a scouting problem or a raw-talent problem. It's a lever problem — nobody visible is pulling evaluation discipline, nobody visible is pulling recruitment speed, and nobody visible is pulling structural reform, all at the exact moment a rival federation is actively pulling lever two against four of Nigeria's own eligible players.

## The twist that keeps us honest

We flagged this in the teaser and it still matters here: even Italy, with real pipeline discipline and a genuinely well-run *oriundi* operation, is not automatically at the 2026 World Cup. They're in the playoffs — same position they've been stuck in before. Pool depth, even *well-managed* pool depth, isn't destiny. It buys options. It doesn't buy results by itself.

Which is exactly why this matters more, not less, for Nigeria. If even the best-run version of lever two doesn't guarantee a trophy, then the case for Nigeria isn't "run a perfect pipeline and results will follow automatically." It's more modest and more urgent than that: right now, on the evidence, nobody is running *any* of the three levers in a sustained, visible way, against a player pool that — by our own count, sourced and verified, dupes and overlaps checked — is deep enough that the constraint clearly isn't the players. We're not claiming this newsletter is the process Nigeria needs. We're claiming someone should be running one — on any of the three timelines, ideally more than one at once — and until they are, we're the closest thing keeping score.
